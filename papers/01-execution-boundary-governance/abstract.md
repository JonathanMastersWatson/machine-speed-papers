# Abstract — Execution-Boundary Governance

Current AI governance frameworks focus on policy, oversight, documentation, transparency, and post-execution accountability. As autonomous systems gain the ability to execute transactions, invoke tools, modify records, move funds, and trigger physical actions, governance must operate within finite latency budgets. This paper proposes Execution-Boundary Governance (EBG), a framework that distinguishes interpretive governance from executable governance and introduces the Executable Governance Kernel (EGK) — a minimal, deterministic, tamper-resistant mechanism operating at the final control point preceding autonomous action. The paper advances seven claims. First, existing AI governance frameworks contain no latency model and cannot distinguish between a system that prevents an inadmissible action and a system that documented one. Second, governance effectiveness is formally bounded by a latency condition: governance latency must be less than action latency, or governance becomes observational rather than controlling. Third, human institutions subsidized governance complexity with human decision latency — a subsidy that autonomous systems cancel. Fourth, the transition from interpretive to executable governance requires governance compilation: the transformation of human-readable policy into machine-enforceable constraints before the execution boundary is reached. Fifth, the kernel enforcing those constraints must satisfy five properties — complete mediation within defined consequence scope, determinism, latency compatibility, tamper resistance, and evidence generation — and must be minimal rather than layered. Sixth, liability naturally converges on the execution boundary as the last point at which control was possible. Seventh, insurance underwriting of autonomous systems will require execution-time evidence — contemporaneous proof of governance presence — rather than post-hoc documentation. These claims are derived from first principles and supported by convergent independent developments across governance theory, cybersecurity, safety engineering, financial market design, edge infrastructure, liability doctrine, and insurance underwriting. The paper does not argue for the removal of human oversight. It argues for its correct positioning — upstream of the execution boundary, where interpretation belongs, rather than at the boundary itself, where only enforcement is possible. The constraint the paper identifies is not invented. It was discovered. It exists whether or not it is instrumented. A note on human oversight This paper does not argue that human oversight should be removed from autonomous systems. It argues that human oversight must be correctly positioned. Interpretive governance — the deliberative, contextual, judgment-based governance that courts, committees, and compliance teams perform — belongs upstream of the execution boundary, where it can do the work it was designed to do. At the execution boundary itself, the time for interpretation has passed. What remains possible is enforcement. The paper’s argument is about sequence, not substitution.
