# Abstract — The Execution Surface

Compute is moving outward. That argument is over, and this paper does not make it. It starts from the condition itself and asks what kind of thing is being built. The answer is a planetary execution surface: a continuous layer of local compute running from gigawatt campuses down to milliwatt endpoints, thickest wherever consequences occur. It is being assembled now, segment by segment, by thousands of unrelated parties — municipalities meeting statutory duties, retailers avoiding bandwidth costs, manufacturers preventing downtime, remote operators with no alternative, and professionals who cannot send confidential work to somebody else’s model. Four findings follow from describing it rather than arguing for it. First, nobody deploys any of this hardware in order to sell compute. Every box is paid for by a cost avoided or an obligation met, so the capital is not speculative and the surface cannot crash the way the fibre build-out crashed. Spare capacity is a byproduct, and there is an enormous amount of it, because every node is sized for its owner’s peak and peaks are brief. Second, distributed execution does not fail — markets for distributed execution fail. A decade of well-funded attempts splits on one question: did the arrangement require somebody to rely on work done inside a stranger’s machine? Every failure did. Every success had a single owner holding both the hardware and the workload. Third, two experiments have already tested this at scale, from opposite directions. Carrier edge computing had the best-sited facilities, trusted operators and no counterparty risk, and no market formed. Decentralised compute marketplaces had auctions, enormous supply and discounts of fifty to eighty percent, and production work stayed away. Each diagnosed a different missing piece and each built it. Neither could tell a buyer what had happened inside the machine. Fourth, the surface becomes a mesh only when three layers are present — the three the earlier papers in this series derived independently. People compile policy upstream. A deterministic Executable Governance Kernel sits at the boundary. An independent witness runs alongside it. The relationship between the last two is specific and easy to invert: the kernel decides whether an action may proceed, the witness records what it can observe, and the witness never gates anything. Without all three, density is not connection, and what remains is a very large number of capable machines each serving one party. The paper stops at that gap. What closing it would be worth, how such capacity would be priced, and whether a market for it clears are the subject of the companion paper on machine economics. Preliminaries Vocabulary note. This paper uses the terminology established across Papers 1–4 of this series: execution boundary, Executable Governance Kernel (EGK), admissibility, independent witness, policy compilation, Compiled Region. It names no implementation of any of them. An EGK is an architectural category in the way a firewall or a hypervisor is a category; the paper argues the category is necessary and says nothing about which one. Prior papers in this series.
